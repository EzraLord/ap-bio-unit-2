Cell Structure and Function

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Every organelle question on the AP exam is a structure → function question. Naming the organelle earns nothing; linking a physical feature to the job it makes possible earns the point.

The secretory pathway

  1. Ribosome on rough ERprotein is made and threaded into the ER lumen
  2. Rough ER lumenprotein folds
  3. Transport vesiclebuds off the ER
  4. Golgi complexmodified, tagged, sorted
  5. Secretory vesiclebuds off the trans face
  6. Plasma membraneexocytosis releases the protein

The endomembrane system is the nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, and plasma membrane. Its parts are linked by vesicle traffic. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not in it: they have their own DNA and ribosomes, and they came from endosymbiosis (2.10).

Where students lose points

  • “Mitochondria make energy.” Energy isn't made. Say mitochondria convert the chemical energy in organic molecules into ATP through aerobic respiration.
  • “The Golgi makes proteins.” Ribosomes make proteins. The Golgi modifies, sorts, and packages them.
  • Putting mitochondria or chloroplasts in the endomembrane system.
  • Naming a structure without its function. “Cristae” earns nothing. “Cristae increase the surface area of the inner membrane, so more electron transport chain proteins and ATP synthase fit, and more ATP can be made” earns the point.

    Show answer

  2. Which structures make up the endomembrane system, and which major organelles are not part of it?

    Show answer

    Show answer
    It is folded into cristae, which increases surface area for electron transport chain proteins and ATP synthase. It also separates the matrix from the intermembrane space, so an H+ gradient can build up and drive ATP synthase.

  4. Name two functions of the smooth ER and a cell type where it is abundant.

    Show answer
    Lipid synthesis (phospholipids, steroids), detoxification of drugs and poisons, and Ca2+ storage. Abundant in liver cells, steroid-making cells (testes, adrenal), and muscle cells.

    Show answer

  6. Why don't lysosomal enzymes do much damage if they leak into the cytosol?

    Show answer

Cell Size

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

A cell exchanges materials (nutrients, O2, wastes, heat) across its surface, but its need for exchange comes from its volume. As a cell grows, volume rises faster than surface area, so the surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) falls, and exchange can't keep up with demand.

Formulas (given on the AP formula sheet)

CubeSA = 6s² V = s³ SA:V = 6/s
SphereSA = 4πr² V = (4/3)πr³ SA:V = 3/r
Rectangular prismSA = 2(lw + lh + wh) V = lwh
CylinderSA = 2πr² + 2πrh V = πr²h
Cubes: surface area grows as s², volume as s³

How living things beat the ratio

  • Stay small, or divide: most cells are 1–100 µm across.
  • Change shape: long, thin, or flat cells keep every point close to the surface (neurons, red blood cells, epithelial cells).
  • Fold the membrane: microvilli, root hairs, cristae, thylakoids. More membrane for the same volume.
  • Organism level: alveoli, villi, gills, and branched roots add exchange surface; circulatory systems carry material the rest of the way.
  • Heat: a large animal has a low SA:V and loses heat slowly per gram of body mass; a small animal loses heat quickly and needs a higher metabolic rate per gram. This is why elephants use large, thin ears to shed heat.
Practice set: SA:V (try before opening each answer)
  1. A cube-shaped cell has sides of 4 µm. Find SA:V.
    AnswerSA = 6(4²) = 96 µm²; V = 4³ = 64 µm³; SA:V = 96/64 = 1.5 µm⁻¹.
  2. A spherical cell has a radius of 3 µm. Find SA:V.
    AnswerSA = 4π(9) = 113.1 µm²; V = (4/3)π(27) = 113.1 µm³; SA:V = 1.0 µm⁻¹ (check: 3/r = 3/3).
  3. Which exchanges more efficiently: a cube with 2 µm sides or a sphere with a 1.5 µm radius?
    AnswerCube: 6/2 = 3.0 µm⁻¹. Sphere: 3/1.5 = 2.0 µm⁻¹. The cube has more membrane per unit of volume.

Where students lose points

  • “Big cells have less surface area.” They have more total surface area. They have less surface area per unit volume.
  • Stating a ratio change with no consequence. A complete answer: size ↑ → SA:V ↓ → less membrane per volume → exchange too slow to meet the cell's metabolic needs.
  • Dropping units. SA:V has units of 1/length (µm⁻¹).

Plasma Membrane

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

The membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates, described by the fluid mosaic model: a fluid bilayer (components drift sideways) with a mosaic of proteins scattered through it.

Phospholipids

Amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. In water they form a bilayer on their own, heads facing the water on both sides, tails hidden inside. That hydrophobic core is what makes the membrane selectively permeable.

Proteins

Integral (transmembrane) proteins span the bilayer. The section inside the bilayer has nonpolar amino acids that interact with the fatty acid tails; the sections facing water have polar and charged amino acids. Peripheral proteins sit loosely on a surface.

Jobs: transport (channels, carriers, pumps), receptors for cell signaling, enzymes, cell–cell recognition, and anchoring to the cytoskeleton or extracellular matrix.

Fluidity

  • Unsaturated tails have C=C double bonds that make kinks, which stop tight packing → more fluid. Cold-adapted organisms have more of them.
  • Saturated tails are straight and pack tightly → less fluid.
  • Cholesterol (animal cells) is a fluidity buffer. At high temperature it restrains phospholipid movement; at low temperature it keeps phospholipids from packing too tightly.
  • Low temperature → less fluid. Membrane proteins need fluidity to move and change shape.

Carbohydrates

Glycoproteins and glycolipids face the outside of the cell and work in cell recognition (for example, ABO blood types and immune self-recognition). They face outward because carbohydrates are attached inside the ER and Golgi lumen, and the lumen side becomes the outer face after exocytosis.

Where students lose points

  • “Cholesterol makes membranes more fluid.” It moderates fluidity in both directions.
  • Mixing up heads and tails. Heads are hydrophilic (they face water); tails are hydrophobic (they face each other).
  • “Membrane proteins flip between layers.” Movement is lateral, within one layer. That keeps the two faces of a membrane different.

  5. Which side of the membrane do glycoproteins face, and what do they do?

    Show answer
    The outside (extracellular side). They work in cell–cell recognition, such as blood types and immune self-recognition.

Membrane Permeability

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

The hydrophobic core decides what crosses without help. Rank molecules by charge first, then polarity, then size.

  1. Small nonpolar: O2, CO2, N2, steroid hormones. Cross freely by simple diffusion.
  2. Small polar, uncharged: H2O, urea, glycerol. Cross slowly in small amounts. (Water moves much faster through aquaporins.)
  3. Large polar: glucose, amino acids. Essentially cannot cross without a transport protein.
  4. Ions: Na+, K+, Cl−, H+, Ca2+. Blocked almost completely, even though they're tiny. Charged particles are surrounded by water and cannot enter the nonpolar core.

Cell walls

OrganismWall made of
PlantsCellulose
FungiChitin
BacteriaPeptidoglycan
ArchaeaOther polysaccharides and proteins (no peptidoglycan)
AnimalsNo cell wall

Cell walls add a structural boundary outside the plasma membrane. They are not selectively permeable (water and small solutes pass through), but they resist expansion, which protects walled cells from bursting in hypotonic surroundings.

Where students lose points

  • Explaining permeability with size alone. Na+ is smaller than glucose and still crosses worse, because charge is the bigger barrier.
  • Calling the cell wall selectively permeable. The plasma membrane is; the wall is not.
  • Calling water nonpolar. Water is polar; it crosses the bilayer only because it is very small.

    Show answer

  4. Is the cell wall selectively permeable? What does it do?

  5. Why can steroid hormones enter cells without a transport protein?

    Show answer
    Steroids are nonpolar lipids, so they dissolve in the hydrophobic core and diffuse straight across.

Membrane Transport

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Cells keep concentrations different on the two sides of their membranes. Those differences (gradients) are a form of stored potential energy. Moving something down its gradient releases energy; moving it up requires energy.

Passive transport

Net movement from high to low concentration. No metabolic energy (ATP) is spent. Includes simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and osmosis. Continues until equilibrium, when movement is equal in both directions (molecules still move; net movement is zero).

Active transport

Net movement from low to high concentration, against the gradient. Requires energy and membrane proteins (pumps). Uses ATP directly (primary) or the energy of another gradient (secondary, see 2.8).

Bulk transport (requires energy)

  • Endocytosis: the plasma membrane folds inward and pinches off a vesicle. Phagocytosis engulfs particles or cells (macrophages eating bacteria); pinocytosis takes in fluid; receptor-mediated endocytosis takes in specific molecules that bind receptors (cholesterol-carrying LDL).
  • Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside (insulin, digestive enzymes, neurotransmitters). The vesicle's membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.

Where students lose points

  • “Passive transport uses no energy.” It uses no ATP. The energy comes from the gradient itself.
  • “At equilibrium, molecules stop moving.” Net movement stops; molecules keep moving both ways at equal rates.

  1. Define passive and active transport using gradient direction and energy.

    Show answer
    Passive: down the concentration gradient, no ATP. Active: against the gradient, requires energy (ATP directly or another gradient) and a membrane protein.

  5. Why is a concentration gradient a form of energy?

    Show answer
    It's potential energy. As molecules move down the gradient, that energy can be harnessed, for example by cotransporters or ATP synthase.

Facilitated Diffusion

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Passive transport through proteins, for things the bilayer blocks: ions and large polar molecules. Always down the gradient, no ATP.

Channel proteins

Hydrophilic pores. Very fast. Often gated: they open in response to a ligand binding (ligand-gated) or a change in membrane voltage (voltage-gated). Ions flowing through channels shift charge and set up the membrane potential.

Aquaporins are water channels. They greatly increase the rate of osmosis. They never change its direction, which is set by water potential.

Carrier proteins

Bind a specific solute, change shape, and release it on the other side (for example, GLUT glucose transporters). Because there are a limited number of carriers, the rate levels off at high concentration: the carriers are saturated.

Where students lose points

  • “It uses a protein, so it's active.” Proteins don't mean energy. Direction relative to the gradient decides.
  • Explaining a plateau with “the cell is full” or “the gradient disappears.” The answer is saturation: every transporter is occupied.

  1. Compare channel proteins and carrier proteins.

    Show answer
    Channels: hydrophilic pores, often gated, very fast. Carriers: bind the solute and change shape, slower, and saturate at high concentration. Both are passive in facilitated diffusion.

  2. What do aquaporins change about osmosis, and what don't they change?

  3. Why does carrier-mediated transport level off at high concentrations?

  4. Name two ways an ion channel can be gated.

Tonicity and Osmoregulation

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential: from the side with fewer dissolved particles toward the side with more. This is the most-tested topic in the unit.

Water potential (given on the formula sheet)

Water potentialΨ = Ψp + ΨsUnits: bars or MPa (1 MPa = 10 bars). Pure water in an open container: Ψ = 0.
Solute potentialΨs = −iCRTi = particles per formula unit (sucrose, glucose 1; NaCl 2; CaCl2 3). C = molarity (mol/L). R = 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K). T = °C + 273.
  • Ψs is 0 for pure water and negative once solute is added. More solute → more negative.
  • Ψp is 0 in an open beaker. In a turgid plant cell it is positive, from the wall pushing back. In a plasmolyzed cell it drops to 0.
Outside solution is…Net water movementAnimal cellPlant cell
Hypotonic (less solute)Into the cellSwells, may lyseTurgid (the healthy state)
IsotonicNo net movementNormalFlaccid (limp)
Hypertonic (more solute)Out of the cellShrivels (crenates)Plasmolyzed: the membrane pulls away from the wall

Worked example

Ψs of 0.15 M NaCl at 20°C:

Ψs = −(2)(0.15 mol/L)(0.0831 L·bar/mol·K)(293 K) = −7.3 bars

In an open beaker Ψp = 0, so Ψ = −7.3 bars. A plant cell with Ψ = −5 bars placed in it would lose water (−5 → −7.3).

Osmoregulation

  • Contractile vacuoles in freshwater protists (Paramecium) collect incoming water and expel it. This uses ATP. The more hypotonic the water, the faster they contract.
  • Central vacuole in plants holds water and solutes and creates turgor pressure against the wall.
  • Tonicity only counts solutes that can't cross the membrane. A solute that crosses freely (urea for red blood cells) ends up on both sides and doesn't hold water back.
Practice set: water potential (paper and calculator)
  1. Ψs of 0.5 M glucose at 20°C?
    Answer−(1)(0.5)(0.0831)(293) = −12.2 bars.
  2. Ψs of 0.2 M CaCl2 at 25°C?
    Answeri = 3. −(3)(0.2)(0.0831)(298) = −14.9 bars.
  3. A plant cell has Ψs = −7 bars and Ψp = +3 bars. It sits in a solution with Ψ = −6 bars. Which way does water move?
    AnswerCell Ψ = +3 + (−7) = −4 bars. Water moves from −4 to −6: out of the cell.
  4. At the same temperature, which has the higher water potential: 0.1 M NaCl or 0.15 M sucrose?
    AnswerNaCl: i·C = 0.2. Sucrose: i·C = 0.15. Fewer particles means less negative Ψs, so 0.15 M sucrose has the higher water potential. Water would move from the sucrose side to the NaCl side.
  5. A plant cell reaches equilibrium in pure water with Ψs = −5 bars. What is its Ψp?
    AnswerAt equilibrium Ψcell = Ψwater = 0, so Ψp = +5 bars.

Where students lose points

  • Saying water moves “from high concentration to low concentration” without saying of what. Say water moves from high to low water potential, or toward the higher solute concentration.
  • Using °C instead of kelvin, using i = 1 for a salt, or dropping the negative sign.
  • Saying solutes move by osmosis. Osmosis is water only.
  • Saying equilibrium means equal solute concentrations. It means equal water potential; in a plant cell, Ψp makes up the difference.

  5. A plant cell in pure water stops gaining water while it still has more solute than the water. Why?

    Show answer
    As water enters, the wall pushes back and Ψp rises until the cell's total Ψ equals 0, the same as the water. Equal water potential, not equal solute concentration, stops net movement.

  6. What is a contractile vacuole, and where is it most active?

    Show answer
    An organelle in freshwater protists that collects excess water and expels it, using ATP. Most active in the most hypotonic (freshest) water.

The Na+/K+ ATPase

  1. The pump, open to the cytosol, binds 3 Na+.
  2. ATP is hydrolyzed; the pump is phosphorylated and changes shape, opening to the outside and releasing Na+.
  3. It binds 2 K+ from outside; the phosphate is released.
  4. The pump returns to its original shape and releases K+ inside.

Both ions move against their gradients (Na+ is high outside, K+ is high inside). Each cycle exports one net positive charge, which helps keep the inside of the cell negative: the membrane potential. The Na+ gradient it builds then powers cotransporters.

Where students lose points

  • Saying the cotransporter uses ATP. It uses the energy of the other solute moving down its gradient.
  • Missing a delay. If an effect appears after a lag, a stored gradient is running down.
  • Justifying “active transport” without both key phrases: against the concentration gradient and requires energy (ATP).

    Show answer

2.9

Cell Compartmentalization

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Internal membranes split the eukaryotic cell into compartments. When a question asks why, give one of these three reasons and connect it to the example in the stem.

  1. Separates incompatible reactions. Digestive enzymes in lysosomes are kept away from the molecules in the cytosol.
  2. Keeps a local environment. Each compartment has its own pH, ions, and enzymes. Lysosome pH ~5 vs cytosol ~7.2; the ER lumen for protein folding; the matrix for the Krebs cycle. Reactants are concentrated, which speeds reactions.
  3. Adds membrane surface area for membrane-bound reactions, and lets gradients form across a membrane: cristae (electron transport and the H+ gradient for ATP synthase), thylakoids (light reactions), and the extensive ER.

Where students lose points

  • “Compartmentalization keeps things organized.” Too vague for credit. Name which reactions are separated or which condition is maintained.
  • Forgetting that membranes are what allow gradients (like H+) to exist at all.

  1. Give three advantages of compartmentalization, with an example of each.

    Show answer
    Separates incompatible reactions (lysosomal enzymes kept from the cytosol). Maintains local conditions (lysosome pH 5; ER lumen for folding). Adds membrane surface area (cristae, thylakoids).

  2. How does a lysosome keep its pH low?

2.10

Origins of Cell Compartmentalization

25 key terms · word list and 50 quick questions

Endosymbiotic theory: mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by an ancestral host cell and not digested. Over time host and guest became dependent on each other.

  • Mitochondria came first, from an aerobic bacterium. Every eukaryote has or had mitochondria.
  • Chloroplasts came later, from a photosynthetic cyanobacterium engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria. That's why only some eukaryotes have them.
  • The endomembrane system (nuclear envelope, ER) most likely came from infoldings of the plasma membrane, not from endosymbiosis.

Evidence (know at least four)

  1. Double membrane, consistent with engulfment.
  2. Circular DNA of their own, like bacteria (the nucleus has linear chromosomes).
  3. 70S ribosomes like bacteria (the cytosol has 80S). Antibiotics that target bacterial ribosomes also inhibit protein synthesis inside mitochondria and chloroplasts.
  4. Division by a fission-like process, independent of the cell's own division.
  5. DNA sequences closest to specific bacterial groups; bacteria-like genes that were transferred to the nucleus.
  6. Similar size to bacteria.

NucleusNo; DNA in a nucleoid regionYes, double membrane
Membrane-bound organellesNo, but some have internal membrane regions with specialized functions (cyanobacterial thylakoids)Yes
Ribosomes70S80S in cytosol; 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts

Where students lose points

  • Citing evidence every cell has (“mitochondria make ATP,” “they have phospholipid membranes”). Evidence must be shared specifically with bacteria.
  • Applying endosymbiosis to the ER or Golgi.
  • Reversing direction (“cyanobacteria evolved from chloroplasts”).

Reader's desk: how free responses are scored

Each point in a scoring guideline is tied to a task verb. Doing more than the verb asks rarely earns more; doing less always loses the point. A statement that contradicts a correct one in the same part can cancel the credit.

VerbWhat earns the pointExample
IdentifyName it. No explanation needed.“The independent variable is sucrose concentration.”

Experimental design vocabulary

  • Independent variable: what the researcher changes. Dependent variable: what is measured.
  • Negative control: a group where no effect is expected (distilled water; no treatment). Positive control: a group with a known effect.
  • Controlled variables: held constant (temperature, time, volume, tissue source).
  • Null hypothesis template: “There will be no difference in [dependent variable] between [group A] and [group B].” Or: “[IV] has no effect on [DV].”

Error bars (±2SE)

  • Bars don't overlap → the difference is likely statistically significant → reject the null hypothesis.
  • Bars overlap → the difference may be due to chance → fail to reject the null.

The graphing point checklist

  1. Axes labeled with the variable and units. Independent variable on x.
  2. Scale and plotting: even intervals, data fill most of the grid, every point accurate.
  3. Graph type: line graph for a continuous independent variable, bar graph for categories. Multiple groups need a key.
  4. Error bars when data are given as mean ± 2SE.

Habits of 4s and 5s

  • Label every part (a), (b)(i)… and answer in order.
  • Use the stimulus's own terms and numbers.
  • Write one clear claim per point; don't hedge with two answers.
  • Don't restate the question. Start with the answer.
